# Node: `opening` 開場設計

Build the first two minutes: the hook that earns attention, the framing that tells the room what kind of conversation this is, and the promise of what they hold at the end. These three do different jobs and are written separately — a strong hook attached to no promise buys interest that decays before the argument starts.

Going wrong looks like an opening that introduces the speaker, lists the agenda, and thanks everyone for their time. Nothing there tells the room why to spend attention, how to listen, or what they are being asked to hold on to. The other failure is subtler: an opening that promises more than the body delivers, which the closing then has to either fake or quietly drop.

## Entry check

Read `docs/deck-consulting/positioning.md` and `docs/deck-consulting/outline.md`. The positioning gives you the 採取姿態 the opening has to be consistent with — a posture that sacrifices the full ask for a scheduled second meeting cannot open at full volume. The outline tells you what the body actually delivers, which is the ceiling on what the promise may claim.

Either can be missing. Say which one, say what it costs — without the positioning the hook has no room to be aimed at and comes out generically punchy; without the outline the promise is a guess the closing will later be audited against — and offer both paths: run the prerequisite first, or work from whatever the presenter can tell you in two minutes. Then do what they choose.

Read `docs/deck-consulting/script.md` if it exists. If a `開場` section is already there, this is a revision: show it, ask which of the three parts is wrong, and rework only that part. When the promise changes, say that the `收尾` section is now stale, because the closing mirrors this promise item for item.

## Steps

1. **Find the presenter's voice before writing anything.** Read their own sentences — the deck's speaker notes, the document they sent, the way they wrote their request to you. Quote back two or three phrases that sound like them and say what you notice: short and blunt, or careful and qualified; first person or institutional. When the material shows no voice at all, ask plainly whether they want it 白話口語 or 正式簡練, with a one-line sample of each, and get the answer before you write pages. Complete when you can name their register in a sentence and they agree it sounds like them.

2. **Name the room's state at second zero.** What are they walking in already believing, already tired of, already suspecting? Derive it from the stakeholder map, not from imagination, and say which part you are inferring. This is the raw material for the hook — an opening is a move from this state to a different one. Complete when the presenter confirms the starting state or corrects it.

   Ask what happened in the meeting immediately before this one, and what else is on the agenda after it. A room arriving from a bad quarterly review is a different room from the same people arriving from a launch, and it changes which hook is survivable.

3. **Offer hooks and let them hear one.** Three to five, each derived from their material, each a short outcome label plus what it costs. For each, one sample sentence in their voice so they can hear whether they would actually say it. Complete when they pick one.

   Places a hook comes from, when you need somewhere to look:

   - **一個他們已經在付的代價** — the cost the room absorbs weekly without having priced it. Strongest when the number is theirs, not yours.
   - **一個和他們認知相反的數字** — safe only when the figure is checkable and the room can check it in the meeting.
   - **一個已經發生、他們還不知道的決定** — a constraint set elsewhere that changes what today's choice even is.
   - **一個具體的人、一個具體的星期二** — a scene from the presenter's own month, at a level of detail no one could have invented.
   - **一個他們自己說過的話** — a line from the last review, quoted back. Cheap, and unusually hard to argue with.

   Derive each from their material. A hook that would work for any company in their industry is a hook you wrote instead of found.

4. **Set the framing.** One or two sentences that tell the room what kind of conversation this is and what you need from them while they listen — a decision today, a reaction to a draft, a heads-up with nothing to approve. This is where the 採取姿態 becomes audible. Complete when the framing names what the room is expected to do, not only what the presenter will cover.

   Read the framing against the posture before moving on. A posture that protects the 可接受底線 cannot be framed as 「今天要請各位拍板」 — the framing would be writing a cheque the closing has to bounce. When the two disagree, say which one you think is wrong and let the presenter choose; do not quietly soften the framing to fit.

5. **Write the promise.** Two to four items the room holds at the end, each stated as something they will be able to do or decide, each traceable to a section that exists in the outline. Read the outline back against it and say out loud which section delivers each item. Complete when every promise item has a section behind it and no section carrying a promise is missing.

   Two is usually right. Four is the ceiling — past that, nobody holds the list, and the closing has more to mirror than ninety seconds allows. If the presenter wants five, the honest reading is that the deck has not decided what it is for yet, and that belongs back in `positioning`.

   The test for each item is whether the room could tell afterwards that they got it. 「了解目前的挑戰」 fails — nobody can say whether they now understand. 「知道這三條路各要花多少錢、風險在哪」 passes, because they either hold the figures or they do not.

6. **Write it out as speech and read it back as speech.** Full sentences, in order, at the length it will actually be spoken. Then read it as if standing up: mark any sentence you cannot finish in one breath and split it at the comma carrying the least meaning; mark any phrase that sounds like it is being read rather than said. Complete when every sentence survives being said out loud.

7. **Write the `開場` section** into `docs/deck-consulting/script.md` in the shape below, then say in one line what changed, and name what makes sense next — usually `closing`, because the promise you just wrote is what the closing has to mirror.

## Craft

**Uncomfortable and relevant beats interesting.** An interesting hook — a striking statistic from another industry, a good story — buys attention and then hands it back; the room enjoys it, relaxes, and re-enters neutral just as the argument begins. A hook that names a cost the room already half-suspects converts attention into stake, because now they have something to lose by not listening. The test is not "would this make them look up" but "does this make the next ten minutes matter to them personally". Discomfort without relevance is just provocation, and it makes the presenter look like they are working the room. Both halves or neither.

**The promise is a contract, and the closing is the audit.** Whatever you promise here, the `closing` node recaps item for item, in the same words. That is the mechanism that makes a presentation feel like one presentation rather than a topic and a request stapled together. It also means over-promising is not free: a promise the body does not deliver leaves the closing with two bad choices, faking it or dropping it, and the room notices the drop more reliably than the presenter expects.

**When the body no longer delivers the promise, fix the promise or fix the body — do not narrow the wording.** After a round of cutting, an opening written against the old outline usually promises one item too many. The tempting move is to soften the wording until it is technically still true. That produces a promise nobody can hold. Instead, either say the item is gone and cut it from both opening and closing, or say that dropping it is what makes the ask unwinnable and the section needs to come back. Name which one you are recommending and why.

**Three parts, three jobs, in this order.** Hook, framing, promise is not an arbitrary sequence: attention has to exist before framing can direct it, and framing has to have set the mode before a promise means anything. An opening that promises before it hooks is a table of contents. One that frames before it hooks is a housekeeping notice. When a presenter wants to lead with the promise because it is the strongest material, the usual fix is that the promise should have been the hook.

**Framing is permission to listen a particular way.** A room that does not know whether it is being consulted or informed defaults to the safer posture, which is critique. Saying 「今天我需要的是一個決定，不是回饋」 costs one sentence and changes what the questions are about for the next twenty minutes. Where the positioning structured the ask as a choice between two options rather than a yes/no, say so here — the room should know from the start that it is choosing, not judging.

**Say the ask exists, not what it is.** The opening names the shape of what is coming — 「最後我會請各位在兩個方案裡選一個」 — so nothing at the end arrives as an ambush. Holding the specific number back is not manipulation; it keeps the room listening to the argument instead of arguing with a figure before they have heard why.

**Open on their problem, not on the presenter's project.** 「今天要跟各位報告 X 專案的進度」 tells the room the next twenty minutes are about someone else's work, and they will listen accordingly. The same content opened on the condition in their world that made the project necessary puts them inside it from the first sentence. This is the single most common rewrite this node performs, and the presenter almost always resists it, because the project is what they have been living in.

**A hook has to be true at the level the room can check.** Senior rooms audit the first claim hardest, because it is the cheapest place to test whether the rest is worth trusting. A number rounded generously, a comparison that does not survive one clarifying question, a scene tidied up for effect — any of these costs more than the attention the hook bought. When the strongest available number is soft, say what makes it soft in the same breath and use it anyway; a hedged true number outperforms a clean false one.

**A room that has heard this pitch before needs the opening to say so.** Attempt three at the same budget does not get to open as if it were attempt one — the room's first thought is "this again", and an opening that ignores it is talking past the only thing anyone is thinking. Name it, say what is different this time in one sentence, and move. The acknowledgement is not weakness; it is the fastest way to get the room's attention off the history.

**Short sentences are a delivery decision, not a style one.** A presenter reading a long subordinate clause out loud runs out of breath in the middle and lands the emphasis in the wrong place. One clause per sentence in the opening especially — this is the passage most likely to be delivered while nervous.

**Ninety seconds is a real budget, and the hook is the part that overruns.** A hook the presenter enjoys telling grows every time they rehearse it, and by the third run-through it has eaten the framing. Say the budget out loud when you write it — roughly forty seconds for the hook, fifteen for the framing, thirty for the promise — not as a rule to enforce but so the presenter can hear where the time went when the opening starts running long.

**The opening is written early and revised last.** It is the passage most sensitive to changes anywhere else: a cut section, a re-ordered storyline, or a narrowed ask all leave it subtly wrong. Whenever another node changes the body, this is the section to re-read first, and the promise is the part to re-read hardest.

## Output shape

Write into `docs/deck-consulting/script.md` under a top-level `## 開場` section. That file is shared: `storyline`, `opening`, `closing`, and `delivery` each own one named section in it. Rewrite only `開場` and leave the others exactly as they are; when your change makes another section wrong, say so rather than editing it.

The `開場` section holds:

- **開場鉤子** — the chosen hook, one line saying what it does to the room, then the script for it.
- **場合定調** — the framing sentences, and one line on what the room is being asked to do while listening.
- **承諾清單** — the two to four promise items, numbered, each in the words the closing will reuse verbatim.
- **開場逐字稿** — the whole opening as continuous speakable script, in the presenter's register, with sparse stage directions where a pause or a slide change matters.

The 承諾清單 is the part other nodes read by name, so keep it as a plain numbered list rather than folding it into the 逐字稿 prose. The closing has to be able to lift the items verbatim.

When the presenter asks for two versions — a full one and a compressed one for when the meeting starts late — write the compressed one as the promise plus one framing sentence, and drop the hook. The hook is the part that needs room; the promise is the part the closing depends on.

Keep the whole opening to what fits in ninety seconds to two minutes when spoken. An opening that runs longer has stopped being an opening and become the first section.
