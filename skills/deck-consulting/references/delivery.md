# Node: `delivery` 表達手法

Take one passage and decide how it gets said: which rhetorical device carries it, which register it sits in, and then the actual words, written to be spoken rather than read. Works on the output of any content node — a section from the outline, a distilled point, a headline that needs unpacking — or on a passage the user pastes in with no artifact behind it.

Going wrong looks like beautiful prose the presenter will not say out loud. It scans well on the page, and in the room it comes out as someone reading. The second failure is a device applied so visibly that the audience recognizes the technique before the point lands, at which point they are watching a performance instead of considering an argument.

## Entry check

Get the passage first. If the user names a section, read it from `docs/deck-consulting/content.md` or `outline.md`; if they paste text, work from that. If neither, ask which passage — this node does nothing useful in the abstract.

Read `docs/deck-consulting/positioning.md` if it exists, for the 採取姿態 and the room. Without it you can still write a passage that sounds good, but you cannot tell whether sounding good is what this room needs — say that plainly, offer to run `positioning` first or to proceed from a two-line description of the audience, and do what they choose.

Read `docs/deck-consulting/script.md` if it exists. The `開場` and `收尾` sections, when present, are where the presenter's register has already been settled; match it rather than establishing a second one. If a `講稿` section already holds a version of this passage, this is a revision: show it and ask what is wrong with how it sounds.

## Steps

1. **Say what the passage is doing.** Proving something, reframing something the room already believes, moving between two sections, softening a number, or making the ask. One sentence. The job selects the device — a passage that has to reframe needs a different move from one that has to prove. Complete when the presenter agrees with the job you named.

2. **Establish the voice from their own words.** Read their material — speaker notes, the document they sent, how they wrote their request — and quote back two or three phrases that sound like them, naming what you hear: blunt or hedged, concrete or abstract, first person or institutional. When the material shows no voice, ask whether they want 白話口語 or 正式簡練 and give a one-line sample of each so the choice is audible, not theoretical. Complete when you can state their register in a sentence and they recognize themselves in it.

3. **Ask which mode they will be in.** Reading aloud from a script, or speaking from it with the script as a safety net. These need different sentences (see Craft), and guessing wrong makes the whole passage unusable. Complete when the mode is named.

4. **Offer devices by effect, not by name.** Three to five, each derived from this passage, each labelled by what it does to the room and costed in one line. Include one sample sentence in the presenter's voice for each, so the choice is made by ear. Complete when they pick one, or describe in their own words what they want it to feel like.

   Moves that tend to survive contact with a business room:

   - **先講一個具體的人，再回到數字** — makes an abstraction feel like it has a cost. Risks the room fixating on the anecdote and treating it as the whole case.
   - **先承認對方最強的反對意見，再說為什麼還是要做** — buys credibility that no amount of evidence buys. Costs the appearance of certainty, which some rooms want more than they want honesty.
   - **把抽象的差距換算成他們熟悉的單位** — 三個人月、一季的預算、兩個客訴週期。Costs exactness, buys a number the room can hold.
   - **先問他們一個他們答得出來的問題** — puts the room in the argument instead of in front of it. Only works when the answer is genuinely theirs, not a set-up.
   - **把結論放在最前面，剩下的都是為什麼** — the fastest way to be understood by a senior room. Costs the build-up, and some material genuinely needs the build-up.

   Label the option by the effect, never by the technique's name. A presenter choosing 「金字塔結構」 is choosing a word; a presenter choosing 「把結論放在最前面」 is choosing what happens in the room.

5. **Write it as speech.** Full sentences at spoken length, in the chosen register and mode. Numbers rounded to what a mouth can say — 「將近三成」 rather than 「28.7%」 — unless the exact figure is itself the point. Connectives at the front of the sentence, so the presenter knows where a sentence is going before committing to it. Complete when the passage exists as continuous script with no bullet fragments left in it.

6. **Read it back as speech, not as prose.** Say it. Mark every place you ran out of breath, stumbled on a consonant cluster, or heard yourself acting. Split the long sentences at the comma carrying the least meaning. Complete when the passage can be read straight through out loud without a stumble.

   Written connectors have spoken equivalents; reach for the spoken one every time:

   | 書面 | 口語 |
   |---|---|
   | 基於上述分析 | 所以 |
   | 綜上所述 | 講到這裡，總結一句 |
   | 值得注意的是 | 這裡有一點要提醒 |
   | 換言之 | 換句話說 · 也就是說 |
   | 就此而言 | 以這件事來說 |
   | 有鑑於 | 因為 |
   | 進行評估 | 評估 |
   | 予以考量 | 考慮進去 |

   The pattern generalizes past the table: two-character verbs beat four-character ones, and any phrase that exists only to signal that a paragraph is turning can be replaced by a pause.

7. **Mark the stage directions sparingly.** Where to pause, where a slide changes, where to stop talking and let the room respond. Three or four marks in a passage, not one per line — a script dense with directions gets read as a script. Complete when every mark earns itself.

8. **Write the `講稿` entry** into `docs/deck-consulting/script.md` in the shape below, then say in one line what changed and which passage is the obvious next one.

## Craft

**A device is a tell the moment the audience can name it before the point lands.** The rule of three, the rhetorical question answered by the speaker, the long dramatic pause — these are recognizable at the moment of use, and recognition converts a listener into a spectator. Someone watching a performance is not weighing an argument; they are assessing a performer, and the assessment is rarely generous in a business room. Choose the device that disappears: the one the audience experiences as the speaker simply being clear. Concrete before abstract disappears. A specific person's Tuesday disappears. Naming the strongest objection before making the case disappears. Triads and rhetorical questions almost never do.

**The device has to be inside the presenter's range.** A move that works for a confident speaker in front of a friendly room is a liability for a nervous one in front of a skeptical one, and the same words land completely differently. When the positioning shows a hostile or senior room, weight the options toward moves that survive being delivered flatly — those are the ones that still work when the presenter's voice goes tight.

**Reading aloud and speaking from a script need different sentences.** A reading script tolerates subordinate clauses and precise figures, because the eye tracks them and the mouth follows. A speaking script cannot: it needs one clause per sentence, the number rounded, the connective at the front, and the key phrase repeated rather than referred back to as 「這一點」. Written in the wrong mode, a passage does not merely underperform — the presenter loses their place mid-sentence and never fully recovers, which is why this decision comes before the writing rather than after.

**Emphasis lives in sentence position, not in adverbs.** 「非常」「極為」「相當」 add syllables and subtract force; the room discounts them automatically. Move the word you want heard to the end of its sentence and stop the sentence there. The pause after it does the work the adverb was trying to do.

**Repetition reads as padding and hears as structure.** Prose edits out a repeated phrase; speech needs it, because the listener has no way to look back. Saying the same three-word phrase at the head of three consecutive points is invisible to the ear as repetition and audible as shape. This is one of the few places where good writing and good speaking genuinely diverge — trust the ear.

**One device per passage.** Two stacked moves cancel: the audience notices the pattern of moves rather than any single one, and the passage starts to sound worked. If the passage seems to need two, it is usually two passages, and splitting it is the better answer than layering.

**Match the device to the slide, not only to the words.** A passage delivered over a dense table cannot carry a story — the room is reading. Over a single number on a blank slide, a story is the only thing that fills the silence. Ask what is on screen while this is said; when the answer is "a slide that fights the script", say so, and offer either a different device or a note for `slidecheck`.

**Where the passage is already good, say so and leave it.** A presenter's own blunt sentence usually beats a polished replacement, because it is the one they will actually say. Rewriting it into something more elegant is the most common way this node makes a deck worse.

## Output shape

Write into `docs/deck-consulting/script.md` under a top-level `## 講稿` section. That file is shared: `storyline`, `opening`, `closing`, and `delivery` each own one named section in it. Rewrite only `講稿` and leave the others exactly as they are; when a rewrite here contradicts the `開場` promise or the `收尾` ask, say so rather than editing those sections.

`講稿` accumulates one entry per passage, each under a sub-heading naming the passage as the outline names it, so entries stay addressable as the deck changes. Each entry holds:

- **這段的任務** — one line: what this passage has to do to the room.
- **採用手法** — the chosen device, named by effect, with one line on what it costs.
- **語域** — the register and the mode, 朗讀 or 脫稿, in one line.
- **逐字稿** — the passage as continuous speakable script, with sparse stage directions.
- **口語替換** — any written-to-spoken swaps specific to this presenter, so later passages stay consistent with them.

Entries accumulate rather than replace each other, so a presenter can come back for one passage at a time across several sittings without losing the ones already done.

Keep each entry to the passage's real spoken length. A 講稿 entry longer than the passage takes to say has stopped being a script and become an essay about the passage.
